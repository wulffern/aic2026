WHAT YOU ARE LOOKING AT

A diode biased at a constant current has a voltage across it that falls almost perfectly linearly with temperature. That is the basis of every temperature sensor and every bandgap reference you will build, so it is worth knowing where the slope comes from and how linear “almost perfectly” really is.

The chain is short. The intrinsic carrier concentration ni(T) comes from the density of states and the bandgap, and it is a ferocious function of temperature — look at the log axis. The saturation current goes as ni2. And then

VD = (kT/q)·ln(IC/IS)

puts the two temperature dependences against each other: kT/q rising, ln(1/IS) falling much faster. The result is the falling, nearly straight line in the third panel. The fourth panel is what is left after subtracting the best straight line through it — a couple of millivolts of curvature, and that curvature is exactly the error a first-order bandgap reference cannot correct.

The slope is not a constant of nature; it depends on where the diode is biased, through dVD/dT = (VD − Eg/q − 3kT/q)/T. The familiar −2 mV/K belongs to a diode sitting near 0.7 V. With the script's numbers — 1 µm2 of junction and 1019 doping on both sides — IS comes out extremely small, VD lands near 0.96 V, and the slope is only −0.8 mV/K. The last readout evaluates that formula so you can watch it track the fitted slope as you move the bias. And notice where the fitted line extrapolates to at 0 K: about Eg/q + 3kT/q, which is where a bandgap reference gets both its name and its output voltage.

THINGS WORTH TRYING

  • Compare the three ni models. The rule of thumb — doubles every 11 K — is a straight line on a log axis by construction; the density-of-states calculation is not, because of the T3/2 prefactor. They also disagree on the absolute value: the script's “advanced” model gives 3.7×109 cm-3 at 27 °C, where the accepted figure for silicon is around 1010, because the effective masses it uses are the low-temperature ones. Being a factor of three out on ni is a factor of nine on IS and about 57 mV on VD. Worth knowing before you trust any of them to three digits.
  • Move IC over its whole range. VD moves by 60 mV per decade, as it must, and the slope dVD/dT gets shallower as the bias goes up: a hotter-biased diode is a less sensitive thermometer. Two diodes at different currents give you the ΔVBE that a bandgap adds back in.
  • Watch the max non-linearity readout as you change IC or the area. A few millivolts over the industrial range is typical, and the residual is curved rather than random, which is why curvature correction is a standard trick and why it works.
  • Change the bandgap and watch the 0 K intercept follow it. The script holds Eg constant with temperature; a real bandgap narrows by about 50 mV between −40 and 125 °C, which adds to the residual curvature you are looking at rather than cancelling it.

THE PYTHON

def calc_ni(T):
    mn = (0.98*0.19*0.19)**(1/3)*m0
    mp = 0.81*m0                       # heavy hole
    Nc = 2*np.sqrt(np.power((2*pi*k*T*mn)/(h*h),3))
    Nv = 2*np.sqrt(np.power((2*pi*k*T*mp)/(h*h),3))
    ni = np.sqrt(Nc*Nv)*np.exp(-Eg/(2*k*T))
    return ni*cm3

I_s = q*A*n_i_adv**2*(1/NA*np.sqrt(Dn/tau_n) + 1/ND*np.sqrt(Dp/tau_n))
Vd  = k*T/q*np.log(I_c/I_s)

line = np.polynomial.polynomial.polyfit(T,Vd,1)
vd_lin_err = Vd - (T*line[1] + line[0])

Source: ex/vd.py, based on Streetman. The diffusion constants are fixed at their 300 K values (Dn = 36, Dp = 12 cm2/s), as in the script; the antenna leakage example adds their temperature dependence.
